Chapter 10

Different brains, different tricks

Chapters 1–9 described the universal shape every modern language model follows, one major structural alternative (Mamba2), and the historical lineage that got us here. This closing chapter mops up the remaining variations — different ways models handle who shares what inside attention, position encoding, making a model bigger without slowing every token down, and squeezing more speed out of the hardware itself. No need to memorize the acronyms, just the idea behind each one.

How many "assistants" pay attention at once

Chapter 3 described attention as one spotlight. In reality, a model runs several spotlights side by side per layer — GPT-2 Small uses 12 of them at once, each one allowed to notice a different kind of relationship (one might specialize in "who's the subject," another in "what's the location," etc.). Each spotlight is called a head. The question that varies by model family is: does every single head get its own full notebook entry in the KV cache, or do several heads share one?

ApproachIdeaUsed by, in this project
MHA (Multi-Head)Every head keeps its own full notebook — most thorough, most memory.GPT-2
GQA (Grouped-Query)Heads are put into small groups that share one notebook — a good middle ground.Llama 3.2, Granite 3.0/4.0
MQA (Multi-Query)Every head shares just one notebook — cheapest on memory.Falcon-7B

None of these change what the model can understand — they change how much memory the KV cache from Chapter 6 needs per token, which is why newer, bigger models increasingly favor GQA: it keeps most of MHA's quality while using noticeably less memory per stored token.

Doing the same math without the wasted trips

MHA/GQA/MQA change how much gets stored and moved around. A separate technique called FlashAttention speeds up the attention math itself without changing what it computes at all — same exact result, done faster. Chapter 3's attention step involves a large, temporary grid of "how much does every word attend to every other word" scores. A naive implementation (which is what this educational project uses) writes that entire grid out to memory, then reads it all back to combine it with the Values. For long sequences, that grid gets huge, and shuffling it in and out of memory becomes the actual bottleneck — slower than the arithmetic itself.

Analogy

Imagine calculating a big spreadsheet formula by writing every intermediate column to paper, then reading each one back to compute the next column. FlashAttention is the equivalent of doing the whole calculation in your head in one pass, in small chunks, and only writing down the final answer — same arithmetic, same result, far less time spent on writing and reading. It's a change to how the computation is organized on real hardware, not to the underlying math from Chapter 3 at all.

This project doesn't implement FlashAttention — its attention code favors being easy to read over being fast — but essentially every production inference engine (including real vLLM) uses some form of it, which is why it's worth knowing the name.

Fixed seating chart, or a compass?

Chapter 2 mentioned two ways to tell a model where a token sits in the sentence. GPT-2's fixed numbered-chair approach hits a hard wall at 1,024 tokens — chair 1,025 simply doesn't exist. RoPE, used by Falcon, Llama, and Granite 3.0 in this project, works more like a compass bearing between any two tokens, computed by a formula rather than looked up in a fixed table — which is what makes it possible to stretch a model's usable length well past what it was originally trained on, with some loss of sharpness the further you stretch it. Granite 4.0 goes a step further and uses no explicit position signal at all in most of its layers — because, as the next trick explains, it tracks order a completely different way.

A panel of specialists instead of one generalist

Every layer we've described so far runs its full "thinking" step on every single token. Mixture-of-Experts (MoE), used by Granite 3.0 in this project, tries something different: instead of one big general-purpose thinking step, it keeps a whole panel of smaller specialist sub-networks (Granite 3.0 has 32 of them) and, for each individual token, only consults a handful of the most relevant specialists (8, in Granite 3.0's case) rather than all 32.

Analogy

Like a hospital with 32 specialists on staff, but any one patient only actually sees the 8 most relevant to their symptoms. The hospital as a whole is huge and highly capable, but each individual visit only uses a fraction of that total capacity — which is exactly why Granite 3.0 is described as "1B parameters, 400M active": the full panel is there, but a much smaller slice actually does the work for any given token.

Already covered Granite 4.0's biggest structural difference — replacing most KV-cache layers with fixed-size Mamba2 state-space layers — got a full chapter of its own rather than a paragraph here. See Chapter 8 if you skipped ahead.

Making every number take up less room

Way back in Chapter 2, every fingerprint number was stored with a certain amount of precision — this project always uses 32-bit numbers (called FP32), giving each individual number 4 bytes of storage, whether it's a weight, an embedding, or a Chapter 6 KV cache entry. Quantization is the technique of storing those same numbers with deliberately less precision — 16-bit (FP16), or even 8-bit or 4-bit integers (INT8, INT4) — to shrink memory use, sometimes dramatically.

Analogy

Like rounding $19.9743 to $20 on a receipt. You lose a little precision, but for most purposes the rounded number works exactly as well, and takes far less room to write down. INT4 is a much more aggressive round than FP16 — more precision lost, but up to 8× less storage than FP32 for the exact same numbers.

This matters enormously in practice: it's the difference between needing to fit a model's weights in, say, 28 GB of memory at FP32 versus roughly 7 GB at INT8 for the exact same model — often with barely any noticeable quality loss, because neural networks tend to be fairly tolerant of small rounding errors spread across billions of numbers. Popular real-world methods include GPTQ and AWQ (ways of deciding how to round a model's weights after training) and GGML/GGUF (the quantized format used by llama.cpp). This project always uses plain FP32 — simplicity over efficiency, true to its educational goal — which is a real part of why the practical parameter ceiling on ordinary hardware is smaller here than it would be for a quantization-aware engine running the same models.

The whole journey, recapped

  1. Tokenization — your sentence is chopped into tokens, plain numbers from a fixed vocabulary.
  2. Embeddings — each token becomes a fingerprint capturing meaning, plus a signal for its position in the sentence.
  3. Attention — every token compares its Query against every other token's Key, and pulls in their Value in proportion to the match.
  4. The residual stream — that comparison-plus-private-thinking (FFN) step repeats across many stacked layers, each one adding refinements onto the same running fingerprint rather than starting over.
  5. The generation loop — the model outputs one ranked guess for the next token at a time; the engine picks one, appends it, and loops — prefill once, then decode repeatedly. Modern chat models first wrap your question in a chat template so that loop produces a conversation instead of finishing a sentence.
  6. The KV cache — the engine remembers every earlier token's Key and Value so it never has to reprocess them, at the cost of memory that grows with the conversation.
  7. The inference engine — a context window bounds how much the cache can grow, and a scheduler with block-based paging serves many conversations at once.
  8. Mamba2 — some modern layers replace the ever-growing KV cache with a fixed-size running state, trading some precision for cost that never grows.
  9. The GPT lineage — GPT-3 was pure scale, ChatGPT added instruction tuning and RLHF on top of Chapter 5's chat templates, GPT-4 likely added MoE, and o1 just runs Chapter 5's same decode loop for longer before answering.

And on this page: the smaller variations — MHA/GQA/MQA, FlashAttention, RoPE, MoE, and quantization — that different model families and engines mix into that same overall shape.
