Chapter 4

Writing the answer, one piece at a time

Here's a genuinely surprising fact: the model never writes a whole sentence. It only ever does one thing — guess the single next token — and it does that same one thing over and over, feeding each guess back in as new input.

Step one: reading the whole question at once ("prefill")

Everything from the last two chapters — tokens, fingerprints, attention across 12 layers — happens for our whole sentence in one single pass. This first pass, where the model reads and digests everything you gave it, is called prefill.

But the model's actual output at this point isn't a word. It's something more like a scoreboard: a confidence score for every single one of the 50,257 possible tokens, ranking how likely each one is to be the very next token. Here's the real output from this project's GPT-2 model for our sentence:

Step two: picking one, and looping ("decode")

Now something has to turn that scoreboard into an actual choice. The simplest rule — called greedy decoding — just always takes the #1 ranked token. That's what our example does, which is why the answer comes out as " Paris" every single time you ask it. Chat assistants usually use a slightly looser rule instead, controlled by a setting called temperature: turn it up, and the model will occasionally pick a lower-ranked-but-still-reasonable word instead of always the top one, which is why the same chatbot can phrase things a little differently each time you ask.

Whichever token gets picked is appended to the end of the sentence, and the whole thing runs again — except this time, the model only has to process the one new token (it remembers everything about the tokens before it; more on how in the next chapter). This repeat-one-token-at-a-time phase is called decode, and it's why watching a chatbot "type" is not a visual effect — it's genuinely producing one token, then the next, then the next.

Analogy

It's a bit like predictive text on your phone keyboard, if predictive text were extraordinarily good: it suggests the most likely next word, you (or in this case, an automatic rule) tap it, and then it suggests the word after that, based on everything typed so far including the word you just accepted. Do that enough times in a row and full sentences emerge — one small, confident guess at a time.

For our sentence, the real loop plays out like this:

6342" Paris"
13"."
—stop

Two tokens, and then the model produces a special end-of-sequence token telling the engine "I'm done" — which is how it knows to stop instead of rambling on forever.

A subtlety worth knowing

Notice we asked GPT-2 a statement to finish ("The capital city of France is…"), not a question with a question mark. That's deliberate: plain GPT-2 is a base model from 2019 — a pure autocomplete engine with no idea it's supposed to be having a conversation. It was only ever trained to guess "what word comes next," so it does best when you hand it something to finish, not something to answer. That's genuinely old technology at this point. So how does a modern assistant take an actual question and hold a conversation, using this exact same next-token machinery? That's the rest of this chapter.

How a question becomes something a completion engine can finish

Here's the part that surprises people: ChatGPT, Claude, Llama, Gemini — every modern chat assistant — is still, underneath, running the exact same loop you just watched. Guess one token. Append it. Repeat. Nothing about that mechanism changed. What changed is what actually gets fed in before the loop starts.

Before your question ever reaches the model, the inference engine wraps it in extra text made of special marker tokens — reserved entries in the vocabulary from Chapter 1 that never appear in ordinary writing, set aside specifically to mark "here's where the user's turn starts," "here's where it ends," "here's where the assistant should start replying." This wrapped version is what actually gets tokenized and fed to the model. Here is the real template this project uses for each modern, chat-tuned model it supports, with our question dropped in:

Llama 3.2 — Meta, 2024 (RoPE + GQA, see the bonus chapter)
<|begin_of_text|><|start_header_id|>user<|end_header_id|> What is the capital of France?<|eot_id|><|start_header_id|>assistant<|end_header_id|>
Falcon-7B-Instruct — TII, 2023 (MQA + RoPE)
User: What is the capital of France? Assistant:
Granite — IBM, 2024 (GQA + RoPE, some releases add MoE)
<|start_of_role|>system<|end_of_role|>You are a helpful assistant.<|end_of_text|> <|start_of_role|>user<|end_of_role|>What is the capital of France?<|end_of_text|> <|start_of_role|>assistant<|end_of_role|>

Once wrapped like this, "predict the next token" and "write the assistant's reply" become the same task — because during a second training phase called instruction tuning (on top of the original next-word pretraining), the model was shown enormous numbers of examples in exactly this wrapped shape, each one followed by a helpful reply. It learned that whenever this pattern of marker tokens shows up, a helpful, conversational answer is what should come next — the same next-token guessing from earlier in this chapter, just aimed at a different learned pattern.

Analogy

It's like the difference between handing someone a half-written sentence and handing them a filled-in customer-support ticket with clearly labeled "Customer question" and "Agent response" fields. Both are just text to fill in. But the second one, because of its familiar shape, makes it obvious what kind of thing should go in the blank — and a support agent who has filled in thousands of those tickets will complete it in ticket-reply style without needing to be told to. The marker tokens are the labeled fields; instruction tuning is the agent's experience with that exact shape.

Why GPT-2 still opened this chapter

GPT-2 has no chat template and was never instruction-tuned, which is exactly why it made the clearest first example: there's no wrapping step to explain before you can see the raw prefill/decode loop. Every modern assistant does that same raw loop plus the wrapping and fine-tuning above — it's an addition on top of Chapter 1–4's mechanics, not a replacement for them.

Under the hood The prefill/decode loop and greedy/temperature choice are implemented in generateResponse() in cmd/ask/main.go, calling model.ForwardWithCache(...) from purego/tensor/model.go once for prefill, then once per generated token. Sampling helpers live in purego/tensor/sampling.go and nanovllm/sampling_params.go. The exact chat templates shown above are copied verbatim from formatPrompt() in that same cmd/ask/main.go file.
