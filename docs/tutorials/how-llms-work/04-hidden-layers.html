Chapter 4

How understanding accumulates, layer by layer

Chapter 3 showed attention as a spotlight, repeated across 12 stacked layers. This chapter opens up what's actually happening to each word's "fingerprint" as it passes through that stack — and reveals that attention is only half of what each layer does.

The same notebook page, annotated over and over

Here's the key idea to hold onto: each token's fingerprint from Chapter 2 — that list of 768 numbers, for GPT-2 — is never replaced as it moves through the 12 layers. It's the same page the whole way through. Every layer reads it, figures out a small correction worth making, and adds that correction directly onto the existing numbers. Layer 2 doesn't start fresh from layer 1's output as if it were brand new input — it keeps building on the same running total.

This running total has a name: the residual stream (also called the hidden state). "Hidden" doesn't mean secret or mysterious — it just means it's an internal, in-progress value the model works with, as opposed to the token IDs going in or the final scoreboard coming out.

Analogy

Picture a single index card for the word "capital," and 12 editors sitting in a row. The card never gets copied or replaced — it's physically passed down the row. Each editor reads whatever is currently written on it, thinks for a moment, and adds a short note in the margin — never crosses anything out. By the time the card reaches editor 12, it still has room 1's original observations on it, plus eleven more rounds of annotations layered on top. That fully-annotated card is what finally gets used to guess the next word.

Each layer's second half: thinking alone, after talking together

Chapter 3's attention step is how a word's note-card learns from every other word's note-card. But every layer in this project's models has a second, quieter step right after attention, called the feed-forward network (FFN, sometimes called an MLP). Unlike attention, the FFN looks at only one word's card at a time, with no peeking at anyone else's.

Analogy

If attention is "the room compares notes out loud," the FFN is "each person goes back to their desk and updates their own notes based on what they just heard." Attention shares information across words; the FFN processes what a word now knows, on its own. A transformer layer is always this pair, back to back — gather from everyone, then think alone — repeated 12 times over for GPT-2 Small.

That "think alone" step involves a deliberately non-straightforward calculation called an activation function — without getting into the arithmetic, its job is to let the FFN represent richer, more curved patterns than plain addition and averaging ever could. GPT-2 uses one called GELU; most of the newer models in this project (Llama, Falcon, Granite) use a more modern variant called SwiGLU instead, which tends to squeeze more useful "thinking" out of the same amount of compute — one of several small upgrades that add up across a modern model.

Two different ways to make a model bigger

With this picture in mind, "model size" splits into two genuinely different knobs, and it's worth being able to tell them apart:

  • Width (hidden size) — how many numbers are on each note card. GPT-2 Small uses 768; Llama-7B uses 4096. A wider card can hold more nuance per word, at each and every layer.
  • Depth (number of layers) — how many editors sit in the row. GPT-2 Small uses 12; Llama-7B uses 32. More layers means more rounds of "gather from everyone, then think alone," which tends to build up more sophisticated reasoning.

Real models scale up both together, but they're independent choices — and it's why two models with the same parameter count can behave differently depending on whether they lean wide-and-shallow or narrow-and-deep.

Where we are so far

Our sentence's six fingerprints have now each been annotated twelve times over — once by attention comparing them to each other, once by an FFN thinking about each one alone — per layer. What comes out the other end of layer 12 is what Chapter 5 turns into an actual next-token guess.

Under the hood The residual add pattern and the GELU/SwiGLU switch are implemented in TransformerBlock.Forward() and FeedForward.Forward() in purego/tensor/transformer.go. Hidden size and layer count are the Hidden and NumLayers fields on ModelConfig in purego/tensor/config.go.
