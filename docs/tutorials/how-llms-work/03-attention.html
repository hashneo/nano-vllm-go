Chapter 3

How the model "thinks"

This is the heart of it. The mechanism that made modern AI models possible is called attention, and — despite the intimidating name — the core idea is something you already do naturally when reading a sentence.

Analogy

Read this sentence: "The capital city of France is…" When you get to the word "is", your brain doesn't treat every earlier word as equally important for guessing what comes next. You instinctively give much more weight to "capital" and "France" than to "the" or "of" — those two words are doing almost all the work of narrowing down the answer. That instinctive "which earlier words matter most, right now?" judgment is exactly what attention computes — just as arithmetic, done for every single word, for every single word that came before it.

What's actually inside the spotlight

"Weighs every other word's relevance" is doing a lot of work in that sentence. Concretely, every word's fingerprint gets used in three different roles at once, each with its own name:

  • Query — the question a word is currently asking. "is" carries a query that roughly means "what specific thing am I about to name?"
  • Key — the advertisement every word puts out about what it has to offer. "France" carries a key that roughly says "I'm a country, useful for answering location-shaped questions."
  • Value — the actual content a word hands over, once it's been picked. If "France"'s key matches "is"'s query well, "France"'s value is what actually flows into "is"'s understanding.
Analogy

Like a library reference desk. You arrive with a question (your query). Every book on the shelf has a spine label summarizing what it's about (its key). You scan spines for the best match to your question — that's the "how much attention" score from the diagram above. Whichever books match well, you actually pull off the shelf and read (their value) — books with a poor match stay on the shelf, contributing little or nothing.

A model doesn't do this just once. It runs this whole query/key/value match-up, for every word against every other word, then hands the improved understanding to another identical step — a whole separate round of look-around-and-update. Each of these rounds is called a layer. GPT-2 Small has 12 of them stacked on top of each other; bigger models have many more. What actually happens to a word's fingerprint as it passes through that stack is the whole subject of the next chapter.

Why "attention" was such a big deal

Older approaches to this problem read a sentence strictly left-to-right, one word at a time, and tended to "forget" words that appeared too far back. Attention instead lets every word directly connect to every other word in one step, no matter how far apart they are — which is a big part of why today's models are so much better at handling long, complicated instructions than what came before them.

Under the hood Query/Key/Value projections and the attention math live in purego/tensor/attention.go (plus mqa.go for the multi-query variant). The 12-layer stack for GPT-2 is built in purego/tensor/model.go, using the NumLayers field from NewGPT2Config() in purego/tensor/config.go.
