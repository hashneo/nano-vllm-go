A plain-language walkthrough

How an LLM turns your question into an answer

No prior knowledge of AI needed. By the end, you'll understand — in real terms, not buzzwords — what happens between you pressing "enter" and the words appearing on your screen.

The question we'll follow all the way through

"The capital city of France is …"

Chapters 1–5 follow this exact sentence through a real, working model — a small, older model called GPT-2 that actually runs inside the project this tutorial documents. We're not making up examples; the numbers you'll see are real output from that model's own tokenizer. GPT-2 is deliberately old and simple: it has no chat interface at all, which makes the raw mechanics easy to see with nothing hidden. Chapter 5 then shows, with real code from this project, exactly what modern chat models like Llama, Falcon, and Granite add on top of those same mechanics to turn "finish this sentence" into an actual conversation — and Chapters 6–8 go deep on how those and other modern models actually run efficiently, including a genuinely different mechanism called Mamba2. Chapters 9 and 10 zoom back out: how OpenAI's own models evolved from GPT-2 to today, and the rest of the modern toolkit — FlashAttention, quantization, and more — that didn't fit neatly into the story above.

Before diving in, it helps to meet the three "characters" involved. Every time you ask an AI model a question, three different pieces of software hand the work off to each other:

1. The tokenizer — a translator that turns your English sentence into numbers, because computers can't read words, only numbers.

2. The model — the "brain." A giant pile of learned number patterns (called parameters) that has seen enough text to predict, very well, what word is likely to come next after any given piece of text.

3. The inference engine — the "kitchen manager." The software that actually runs the brain efficiently: remembering what's already been read, juggling multiple people's questions at once, and deciding when to stop generating.

A common misconception is that the "model" does everything. In reality the model is just the recipe/knowledge — it's inert without an engine to run it, and the engine's design has a huge effect on how fast, how long, and how many questions can be answered at once. This tutorial covers both, because this project (nano-vllm-go) implements both from scratch, in the open, with no black boxes.
