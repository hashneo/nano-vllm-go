Chapter 1

Turning words into numbers

A computer chip has never read a single word in its life. It only ever does arithmetic on numbers. So step one, always, is translating your sentence into numbers — and the way that's done is surprisingly simple.

Analogy

Imagine a box of exactly 50,257 pre-printed puzzle pieces. Each piece has a little chunk of text printed on it — some pieces are whole common words like "The", some are just a few letters like "ing", some are a word with a space glued to the front like " capital". Every piece also has a serial number stamped on the back. To "read" a sentence, you find the right sequence of pieces that spells it out, and write down their serial numbers. That list of numbers is all the computer ever actually sees.

This chopping-into-pieces process is called tokenization, and each piece is called a token. The specific method used here — inventing a fixed box of ~50,000 frequently-reused chunks by looking at huge amounts of text and merging the most common pairs of letters together — is called Byte-Pair Encoding (BPE). It's a compromise: a token for every possible whole word would need an impossibly huge box, but a token for every single letter would mean an enormous, slow-to-process list of numbers for even one sentence. BPE lands in the middle — common words get their own single piece, rare words get split into a few pieces.

Doing it for real

Here is the actual, real output of this project's own tokenizer — not a made-up example — when it's given our running sentence. Notice it doesn't split one-per-word exactly; the space before a word is often folded into the next piece, which is a quirk of how this particular vocabulary was built:

464"The"
3139" capital"
1748" city"
286" of"
4881" France"
318" is"

Six words became six tokens this time — but that's a coincidence of this particular sentence, not a rule. A rarer word like "tokenization" itself would likely get chopped into two or three pieces, e.g. something like " token" + "ization", because that exact whole word never earned its own piece in the box.

Why this matters later

Everything downstream — the model's "understanding," the memory it manages, the length limit on a conversation — is measured in tokens, not words or characters. When you hear a model has a "128,000 token context window," this chapter is why: that's the total number of these little numbered puzzle pieces it can hold onto at once, for both your question and its answer combined.

Under the hood This project's tokenizer lives in purego/bpe_tokenizer.go and purego/gpt2_tokenizer.go, and loads the real GPT-2 vocabulary from vocab.json / merges.txt. The numbers above came straight from running that code.
